## Experiment Explanation 
I like to start the experiment by asking the kids what they know about planets, from experience I find a huge variation in the amount people know about them regardless of age. Then ask them, if the Sun is a pretty average star (average mass, average brightness, average temperature), then if it has 8 planets around it do they think other stars should also have planets? Yes!

How do we find these planets? Compare the size of the Earth to the size of the Sun - planets are wayyyyy smaller. Compare the brightness of planets to that of the Sun (either planets in the night sky or just look at the ground, is it brighter or dimmer than the daytime sun?). Clearly it's going to be very difficult to take a picture of a very small very dim thing if its right next to a very big very bright thing.

**Method 1: The Transit Method**
- Turn on the torch and set it pointing at a wall. Explain that the torch is the star, the polystyrene balls on strings are planets, and the patch of light on the wall is what we can see through a telescope.
- Get them to make the planets 'orbit' the star, making sure that the planet passes into the beam of the torch each time. Ask them what they notice happens to the light we see through the telescope - hopefully they point out that the planet is casting a shadow (it is blocking light from the star)
- How does this help us? If we measure the brightness of a star for an extended period of time, we might see a dip in its light - if we see this dip happen again and again at regular intervals we can infer that it's a planet
- When might this not work?
  - Exoplanet orbit has to be aligned with our line of sight (only 0.5% are) - can show this by orbiting the planet in a plane perpendicular to the beam of the torch, it doesn't block any light.
  - Exoplanets could have orbits which take too long for us to see (e.g. Neptune takes 165 years to orbit the Sun, so an alien using the transit method to find planets would have to look at the Sun for two full orbits (330 years) to confirm the existence of Neptune)
- Do scientists actualy do this? Yes! Kepler, TESS, and the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) are dedicated exoplanet survey space telescopes (HWO to be launched in ~2040), and JWST and Roman are able to do this as well, although not their main mission

**Method 2: The Radial Velocity Method**
- Can we come up with a method that works for those other 99.5% of planets the transit method can't detect? Yes!
- Get out the buzzer and attach it to the battery. It is quite loud, so only have it on when actively using it and warn the kids that it's loud, although they're usually fine. To get the Doppler effect, hold the battery in your hand with the buzzer attached, and hold down the string with your thumb on top of the battery where the wire connects, making sure the wire is slack and the string is taut. Then, start swinging the buzzer in a circle being careful not to hit anyone. You should be able to hear the pitch changing even at relatively low speeds.
- Ask them what they notice is happening to the sound - it is getting higher and lower pitched each time the buzzer swings around. Ask them if they've ever noticed when an ambulance drives past them, the siren is higher pitched when it's getting closer and lower pitched when it's getting further away - usually they say yes. This is an example of the Doppler effect.
- This next part depends heavily on their prior knowledge. Have they heard of waves? Do they know sound is a wave? Amazing! If they don't give the example of waves on water, and then that sound is like that but in air (I know that's very inaccurate but I don't think a child who has never heard of a wave before will be amenable to the idea of compressions and rarefactions)
- The distance between two wave crests (or just 'between two waves') is what we call a wavelength. In sound waves, the wavelength tells us the pitch, shorter = higher, longer = lower. As the buzzer swings towards you, the waves get squeezed together -> lower wavelength -> higher pitch, and vice versa for when the buzzer swings away.
- This happens to all waves if their source moves - including light waves coming from a star! For light, shorter wavelengths are bluer, while longer wavelengths are redder.
- What keeps a planet in orbit around a star? Usually they are happy to say it's the star's gravity. But the planet also has some gravity (clearly, since the Earth has gravity), which means the planet can pull a little bit on the star! This is enough to make the star 'wobble' around in a small circle, just like the buzzer was earlier.

**FAQs from the kids**
- Can we take pictures of exoplanets? Yes! But they are very small dots (no surface detail), and only of very big planets orbiting very far away from their star
- What's the nearest exoplanet? Proxima Centauri b, 4.3 LY away ('b' means it is the first planet orbiting the star Proxima Centauri, which is the nearest star to the sun), very close by astronomical standards!
- Can exoplanets orbit black holes? Yes, although I'm not sure if we've discovered any, maybe someone can amend this if we do
- How many exoplanets have we found? Over 6000! Mostly with the transit method, some with the RV method
- How heavy/big can a planet be / is it true that Jupiter is a failed star? The lowest mass a star can be (the mass at which fusion starts) is 13 Jupiter Masses, so that is the maximum / Jupiter is a long way off being a star
