## Experiment Explanation 

A model of the lungs to show how the diaphragm and rib cage cause the lungs to inflate and deflate. 

### Explanation:

Begin with general concepts…

1. Show them the anatomical model, for younger kids ask what they think it is
2. Ask what your lungs are for – answer is usually breathing
3. Get across the concept of 2 functions – getting the air into your lungs, then getting the oxygen from your lungs and into the blood – then say how the model show the first of those.

Then onto how the functional model works…

1. Get them to name the different parts of the model = ribcage and lungs
2. Get them to take a deep breath, and describe what’s happening to their chest as they do it = getting bigger
3. Why is it getting bigger? This is where the confusion usually sets in, because a lot of kids think that the lungs fill up with air and push the chest out. So, need to say that the lungs are just bags, can't do anything on their own to get the air in
4. So, the chest is getting bigger because the ribs are moving up and outwards (moved by muscles)
5. Also, when you want to breathe in, you move the diaphragm, which is the big muscle underneath the ribcage – point it out on the model.
6. So what happens when we move the diaphragm down on the model? Balloons (lungs) fill up with air, and all we’ve done is move the diaphragm. 
7. Why do they fill up? When the ribs are moved up and out, and the diaphragm pulled down, do we have less or more space inside the chest = more space. When we have more space, something has to fill the space, but what? = Air, which comes into your mouth, down your throat, and into your lungs, makes them bigger, to fill up the extra space. (You can talk about pressure if they're old enough to understand, or if they've done the vacuums experiment!)
8. Then breathing out – ribs move down and inwards, and diaphragm moves back up – this is usually a passive process unless you need to breathe harder and faster (e.g. in exercise). Let the diaphragm go on the model – what happens? Balloons (lungs) get smaller, as air is coming out of them. Why? Have made less space inside the chest, so no space for the air in the lungs, so air forced out of lungs. 
11. So overall, breathing is all about changing how much space you have inside your chest, causing air to move in and our of the lung.

### General stuff:

- Point to parts on model and on yourself while talking.
- Nice to demonstrate the lung model alongside the larynx model

### Additional stuff:

1. Gas exchange
- Why do we breathe? = to get air into our lungs. Why? What’s in the air that we need? = oxygen
- Where does the oxygen need to go? = all over the body to all our cells. And why? = to make energy
- How does the oxygen get around the body? = travels in the blood (attached to red blood cells)
- So, the oxygen is in the air, and needs to get into the blood – how? = travels down tubes in the lungs to the edges of the lungs, into little sacs (lung is big sack made up of lots of little sacs = alveoli). Next to the little sacs are blood vessels carrying blood. Oxygen travels from alveoli into the blood and gets carried away to the heart, which pumps the blood (and therefore the oxygen) around the body
- Also, in the alveoli, a gas comes out of the blood, a gas we don’t need = CO2, which gets breathed out

2. If things go wrong
- *Pneumothorax* - When the lung is punctured (e.g. by broken ribs) so it can't take air in. Talk about how even if one lung is down, the other can still work. 
- *Asthma* - Ask if they have it themselves or have friends who have it? The tubes get smaller, making it harder to breathe in and out. Could talk about how salbutamol inhalers ('blue inhalers') have medicine in that makes the muscle around the tubes relax, so the tubes become less constricted and more air can get in. Can be nice to draw a diagram of a normal vs asthmatic airway to show. If kids are keen: could discuss inflammation and how steroid inhalers reduce inflammation, therefore reducing the likelihood of an asthma attack.
- *Hiccups* - show that the diaphragm is right above your tummy. If you eat hot food too quickly sometimes it can irritate the diaphragm leading to involuntary contractions. Other times it can be spontaneous.

## Risk Assessment

### **Hazard: Choking risk from free straws or balloons**

**Description**: Risk of choking on straws or balloons that have detached from the model and additional balloons that are available to repair the model.

**Affected People**: Public

**Before Mitigation**: Likelihood: 2, Severity: 5, Overall: 10 (high)

**Mitigation**: Supervise use of the model. Make sure that any balloons that are not part of the model are put away when demonstrating. In case of accident, call a first aider and encourage child to cough.

**After Mitigation**: Likelihood: 1, Severity: 5, Overall: 5 (medium)

<br/>

### **Hazard: Straw in functional lung model**

**Description**: Transfer of infection from straw if children place it into their mouth.

**Affected People**: Public

**Before Mitigation**: Likelihood: 3, Severity: 2, Overall: 6 (medium)

**Mitigation**: Supervise use of the model. Tell children that the straw is not to be placed in their mouth.

**After Mitigation**: Likelihood: 1, Severity: 2, Overall: 2 (low)

<br/>

### **Hazard**: Holding breath

**Description**: Dizziness from holding breath or breathing too heavily.

**Affected People**: Public

**Before Mitigation**: Likelihood: 2, Severity: 1, Overall: 2 (Low)

**Mitigation**: Warn children not to breathe too heavily or hold their breath. Instruct people to sit on the floor if they feel faint. Call first aider in case of injury.

**After Mitigation**: Likelihood: 1, Severity: 1, Overall: 1 (Low)
